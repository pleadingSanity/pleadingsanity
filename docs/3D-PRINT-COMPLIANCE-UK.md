# Selling 3D prints in the UK — what the law needs (research brief, 15 Nov 2026)

Written for Shane (Pleading Sanity, Leicester) before the printer arrives. Plain summary of official guidance, not legal advice. If in doubt, ask Leicester Trading Standards (contact at the end). Sources are linked at each point and listed at the bottom.

## Short answer
- **Toys are regulated before the first sale.** "Just a side hustle" or "only a few at a market" makes no difference. Anything designed or intended for play by children under 14 is a toy under the Toys (Safety) Regulations 2011. [1]
- **No toy should go live until its paperwork is done**: safety assessment, EN 71 evidence, technical file, declaration of conformity, UKCA or CE mark, and a traceability label. The site enforces this: a "toy" item cannot be published unless `compliance` is true and a technical file reference is filled in.
- **Start with non-toys** (desk items, organisers, phone stands, adult keyrings, Crying Brain merch). These still have to be safe under the General Product Safety Regulations 2005, but they need no UKCA/CE mark, no EN 71 testing and no toy technical file. [5][6]

## 1. Toys (Safety) Regulations 2011 (Great Britain)
Before placing a toy on the GB market, the manufacturer (that is Shane, if he designs or prints it and sells under his name) must: [1]
1. Design and make it to meet the essential safety requirements (physical/mechanical, flammability, chemical, electrical, hygiene).
2. Carry out a **safety assessment** (a written risk assessment of every hazard: small parts, sharp edges, breakage, chemicals).
3. Follow the **conformity assessment**. If harmonised/designated standards (the EN 71 series) are applied in full, the manufacturer can self-assess (internal production control). If standards are not applied or do not exist, a third-party approved body is needed.
4. Draw up a **declaration of conformity** (UK DoC for UKCA, EU DoC for CE) and fix the **UKCA or CE mark** visibly, legibly and indelibly on the toy, a label on it, or its packaging. Very small toys can carry it on a label or leaflet.
5. Draw up the **technical documentation** and keep it and the DoC for **10 years**.
6. Put **traceability** on the toy (or packaging if too small): a type/batch/serial number, plus the manufacturer's name, registered trade name or trade mark, and a contact postal address.
7. Supply **warnings and instructions for safe use** in English.

**UKCA or CE?** For GB, either is accepted indefinitely: the Product Safety and Metrology etc. (Amendment) Regulations 2024 (in force 1 October 2024) continue CE recognition for toys. Northern Ireland and the EU need CE. [1][2][3]

**Coming changes:** the EU's new Toy Safety Regulation (EU) 2025/2509 entered into force on 1 January 2026 but only applies from 1 August 2030; the UK government is running a call for evidence on toy rules. The Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025 lets government make new product-safety rules (including duties for online marketplaces), but those regulations are still proposals. Nothing here changes what Shane must do today. [4][9]

## 2. EN 71 testing (parts 1–3)
- **EN 71-1 (mechanical and physical):** small parts, sharp points and edges, strength of joints, parts that snap off, drop/torque/tension tests, pinch points. Many of these checks can be done and documented by a competent maker with the right gauges and method; writing "passed" without evidence is not enough. [7][8]
- **EN 71-2 (flammability):** mostly relevant to soft toys, costumes and things that are hugged, but still assess it. [7][8]
- **EN 71-3 (migration of certain elements):** heavy metals and other elements leaching from the material, including colourants. Needs a lab. Small makers usually rely on the **filament maker's EN 71-3 certificate, for the exact product and colour** (check it is genuine, in English, current, and covers every colour used), or pay for their own lab test. [7][8][10]
- A filament certificate covers the material only. It does **not** make the finished toy compliant: design, size, age grading and mechanical hazards still have to be assessed. [7][8]

## 3. Small parts and choking (under-3s)
- A toy for children under 36 months must not have, or break into, parts small enough to fit the EN 71-1 small-parts cylinder. Articulated or multi-part prints (flexi dragons, snap-together sets, keyrings with rings) are high risk because joints and thin layers can snap. [7][8]
- If a toy is not suitable for under-3s, it needs the age warning (the standard wording is "Not suitable for children under 36 months" together with the reason, e.g. "small parts, choking hazard", or the official age-warning pictogram). The warning cannot be used to dodge the rules on a toy that is obviously aimed at babies. [1][8]
- Toys with play appeal for under-3s may also need EN 71-3 evidence for that age group, often by lab test. [10]
- PLA is fairly brittle: drop-test the real print and check what breaks off.

## 4. Materials: PLA vs PETG, food contact, colourants
- **PLA:** easy to print in black or white and low odour, but brittle and it softens at about 55–60 °C (hot car, dishwasher). Good for desk items and display pieces.
- **PETG:** tougher, less brittle, more heat-tolerant; a better choice for toys that get dropped, but stringier to print.
- **Food contact:** do not sell FDM prints as food safe. Layer lines trap bacteria, and the filament, colourant and the printer's brass nozzle would all need food-contact evidence. Product copy on the site already says "not for food or drink".
- **Colourants and additives:** black and white pigments vary by brand. Buy filament whose supplier provides an EN 71-3 certificate for the exact colour (for example Bambu Lab's toy-safety-tested range, 3D Filament Warehouse's EN 71-3 range, SpoolSpider PLA). Keep a copy of each certificate in the technical file and note the spool batch number. [10][11][12][13]

## 5. Age labelling
Put the age grade on the listing and the label. On the site every item has an `age_rating`. Non-toys show "14+ · not a toy" so they are clearly not marketed to children. A decorative item that obviously appeals to young children can still be treated as a toy by Trading Standards, so avoid kid-style copy on non-toys. [1][6]

## 6. Technical file (per toy)
Product description and photos; intended age; materials and filament certificates (EN 71-3) with batch numbers; the risk assessment; EN 71-1 and EN 71-2 test records; label artwork and warnings; the signed DoC; batch and traceability records. Update it whenever the design, material, supplier or use changes. Keep it 10 years. [1][7][8]

## 7. Responsible person and traceability
For a toy made and sold in GB by Shane, Shane is the **manufacturer** and carries the duties. His name (or trade name) and a contact postal address must be on the toy, its label or its packaging. An importer or authorised representative is only relevant if someone else makes it outside the UK. Non-toys also need the producer's name and address and a product or batch reference on the product or packaging where reasonable. [1][5][6]

## 8. Product liability insurance
Not a legal requirement for a sole trader, but strongly advisable before selling toys, and many markets and craft fairs ask for public and product liability cover as a stall condition. Under the Consumer Protection Act 1987 the producer is liable for damage caused by a defective product. Get quotes before the first toy sale. (Practical advice, not a cited legal duty.)

## 9. Trading Standards
Leicester City Council Trading Standards gives business advice on product safety: business-regulation@leicester.gov.uk, 0116 454 3200, City Hall, 115 Charles Street, Leicester LE1 1FZ. [14] If a product turns out to be unsafe, a producer must notify the authority and act (withdraw or recall). [5][6] Business Companion (the trading standards guidance site) has free quick guides and a product-safety webinar series. [15]

## 10. Selling online: consumer law
Online buyers can cancel within 14 days of receiving goods (custom or personalised items excepted), and the seller must give clear pre-contract information including cancellation rights; refund within 14 days of getting the goods back. [16][17] The prints page states this. The order flow stores the buyer's name, address and email only to fulfil the order: **the privacy page should get a short line about order data** (not changed in this PR because the privacy page is protected; Shane to approve).

## 11. Tax: HMRC side-hustle thresholds
- **Trading allowance:** up to £1,000 gross trading income per tax year (6 April to 5 April) is tax free, and no need to tell HMRC if that is your only trading income. Making goods to sell, even from a hobby, counts as trading. All trading income adds together. [18][19]
- Over £1,000 gross: register for Self Assessment as a sole trader by **5 October** after the end of that tax year, and either claim the £1,000 allowance or deduct real expenses (filament, printer, electricity, postage), not both. [19][20]
- If Shane already files Self Assessment for Pleading Sanity, print income goes on that return; check with an accountant whether it is part of the same trade.
- **Platforms report to HMRC:** Etsy, eBay and others report sellers with 30 or more sales or about £1,700 (EUR 2,000) or more in a calendar year. Reporting is not a new tax. [21][22]
- Keep records of every sale and cost (the site's order store plus PayPal statements help).

## 12. Are 14+ decorative keyrings, bag charms and flexi prints toys? (added after Shane's £2 first batch)
**Short answer: a flat 2D keyring or bag charm with little play value, sold to adults and teens, is not a toy. A 3D figure, flexi or articulated keyring usually is a toy, whatever the label says.**
- The law: anything "designed or intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14" is a toy. Schedule 1 of the Toys (Safety) Regulations 2011 lists things that are *not* toys, including "decorative objects for festivities and celebrations" (para 2), "products for collectors" marked 14+ that are detailed models or decorative dolls (para 3), and "fashion accessories for children which are not for use in play" (para 20). [23]
- The EU expert-group guidance on decorative products (Guidance Document No 20, which GB enforcers still use as a reference because the GB rules come from the same Directive) says the test is **3D shape plus play value = toy; 2D shape with no or low play value = not a toy**. It gives a key ring with a 3D animal, doll or figure on it as a toy, and a plain flat key ring as not a toy. [24]
- The grey-zone guidance says "a key-ring with a teddy bear attached to it is considered as a toy" and "the reasonably foreseeable use shall prevail over the declaration of intended use". So a label saying "14+, not a toy" does **not** by itself take a figure or flexi out of the toy rules. [25]
- Other things enforcers look at: how it is advertised and to whom, where it is sold, size, price, pictures on the packaging. [24][25] That is why the site has no child imagery, no "kids" copy and no toy styling on non-toys.
- **What this means for the site:**
  - Flat Halloween, snowman, hero, P.S. monogram, hoodie and racerback charms: may go live as 14+ decorative keyrings once real photos, batch and licence are in.
  - Flexi skeleton, ghost, Crying Brain, articulated snowman and pumpkin: **treated as toys**. The code blocks them (`form: 3d` keyring = toy) until a full toy technical file exists (sections 1 to 7 above).
  - A solid, static display sculpture (Crying Brain on a base) is a decorative item, 14+, but keep it chunky and avoid cute character styling.
- "Test product", "first batch" or "only £2" does not exempt anything: the same safety duties apply from the first sale. [1][5]
- **No copies of film or comic characters** (Marvel, DC, Disney, Scream/Ghostface, Jack Skellington): selling them infringes trade marks and copyright, separate from safety law. The server refuses common trademarked names.

## 13. General product safety: what every non-toy still needs (GB)
GB still uses the General Product Safety Regulations 2005 (the newer EU GPSR 2023/988 applies in Northern Ireland and the EU, not Great Britain). [5][26] For each keyring or charm Shane must:
- Only sell products that are safe in normal and reasonably foreseeable use, with warnings where needed (small parts, choking hazard, keep away from under-3s, not for chewing, not food-safe; the site shows these on every card). [5][6]
- **Traceability:** put the producer's name and address and a product reference or batch on the product or its packaging where reasonable. For tiny prints, use a parcel insert card: "Pleading Sanity (Shane Cooper), [postal address], Leicester. Batch B001." The site records a batch code per item and shows it on the card. [5][6]
- Keep records so a bad batch can be traced, and act (withdraw, warn, recall) and tell Trading Standards if something turns out to be unsafe. [5][6]
- If he ever sells into Northern Ireland or the EU, EU GPSR adds an electronic contact address and other online-listing details. [26]

## Safer low-regulation products to start with (non-toys, label "14+ · not a toy")
| Product | Why it is lower risk | Still required |
|---|---|---|
| Phone stand | Adult desk accessory | Safe design (stable, no sharp edges), name and address on packaging or insert, honest copy |
| Desk organiser / pen pot | Adult desk accessory | As above; say "not for food" |
| Cable tidies, headphone hooks, wall hooks (light duty) | Household items | Safe-load statement if it holds weight; don't overclaim |
| Flat 2D keyrings and bag charms, 14+ (Crying Brain, P.S. monogram, Halloween, snowman) | Flat, low play value, fashion/decorative accessory [23][24] | Sturdy steel ring; 14+ and small-parts warnings; batch and maker on an insert; no child imagery or kid-style copy |
| Crying Brain desk sculpture / plaque | Decorative merch | Brand rules (no emoji, brand colours) |
| Plant pots (decorative, with "not watertight" note) | Home item | Honest description |

Avoid until properly checked: anything for babies, anything that looks like a toy or character figure, articulated or flexi animals, food or drink items, items for motorcycles or anything protective, and anything electrical.

## Exactly what Shane must do before any toy, flexi or articulated keyring goes live
1. Pick one simple toy design (one piece, no small or detachable parts, thick walls). Decide the age grade.
2. Buy filament with an **EN 71-3 certificate for that exact product and colour** (black and white). Save the certificate and spool batch numbers.
3. Print samples and run and record **EN 71-1** checks (small-parts cylinder, sharp edge and point checks, drop and torque/tension where relevant) and assess **EN 71-2** flammability. If unsure, pay a test lab or use a maker-compliance service. [7][8]
4. Write the **risk assessment**.
5. Write and sign the **UK (or EU) declaration of conformity**.
6. Design the **label**: UKCA or CE mark, name, postal address, batch number, age warning and safe-use instructions.
7. Put it all in a **technical file** with a reference (e.g. `TF-ROCKET-001`) and keep it for 10 years.
8. Get **product and public liability insurance** quotes.
9. On creator-prints.html: set the toy's real photo (untick mockup), tick "technical file complete", enter the file reference, set status Live. The server refuses any toy without both.
