Know Your Rights

These are the real rules, checked against Ofgem, Citizens Advice, the judiciary and GOV.UK. Every claim links to its source.

Energy bill disputes: complain first, then the Energy Ombudsman

If your bill is wrong, tell your supplier in writing and keep a copy. The supplier must try to fix it within 8 weeks. If they do not fix it, or you get a "deadlock" letter saying they cannot do more, you can take it to the Energy Ombudsman — free, independent, and their decision binds the supplier (it does not bind you). Ofgem regulates the sector but does not resolve individual complaints.

  • Complain to your supplier first, in writing, and keep records.
  • Escalate after 8 weeks or a deadlock letter to the Energy Ombudsman.
  • Citizens Advice consumer helpline: 0808 223 1133 can help you frame the complaint.

Sources: Ofgem — Complain about your energy supplier · Citizens Advice — Complain to the Energy Ombudsman · Energy Ombudsman — How we can help

Prepayment meters: Ofgem's strengthened rules (2024 reforms)

A supplier can only move you to a prepayment meter without your permission as a last resort, and only if Ofgem has approved that supplier's practices. Before any involuntary installation the supplier must make at least 10 attempts to contact you and carry out a site welfare visit. They must not forcibly install one for the highest-risk households — including homes dependent on powered medical equipment, occupants aged 75+ without support, children under 2, severe or terminal illness, or where no one can top up due to physical or mental incapacity. A £30 credit (or equivalent non-disconnection period) must be given at installation. These licence protections (Standard Licence Conditions 28.10–28.13) have been extended and remain in force.

Sources: Ofgem — New rules to install prepayment meters for recovering debt · Ofgem — Extending protections on prepayment meters installed under warrant · Citizens Advice — Stop your supplier installing a prepayment meter

Warrants of entry: what they are and your right to be heard

If you refuse entry, a supplier can apply to a magistrates' court for a warrant of entry — a court order allowing them to enter your home to fit a meter. The supplier must tell you at least 21 days before applying for the warrant, and you can ask to join the hearing (usually by video) to tell the court why a prepayment meter is not suitable for you — for example a health condition or inability to top up. If a warrant is granted it is valid for 28 days. The courts resumed listing these applications in 2024 under stricter directions; suppliers must satisfy the court they have followed the rules.

Sources: Judiciary — Directions on warrants for prepayment meters · Citizens Advice — Warrants and your rights

Court claims for energy debt: respond — never ignore

Most suppliers rarely sue for household energy debt because the meter route is cheaper and their licence requires them to offer affordable repayment first. But if a county court claim form does arrive, respond to it. An unanswered claim becomes a default judgment (CCJ) that stays on your record for six years. Responding lets you propose instalments you can afford. If a CCJ is granted and you do not pay as ordered, the supplier can apply for a warrant of control, and an enforcement agent (bailiff) may get involved — they must send a notice of enforcement giving at least 7 days (often 14 clear days) before visiting, and they cannot force entry to a residential home unless they have previously been let in. At every stage, ask for an affordable repayment plan based on what you can actually pay.

Sources: GOV.UK — Warrant of control · National Debtline — County Court bailiffs · Citizens Advice — Bailiffs and your rights

Tenants: your landlord still has duties

If you rent, your landlord cannot disconnect your energy or force you onto a meter unlawfully. You are the customer of the energy supplier in your name and you have the same Ofgem rights as any other customer. If the property is below the legal energy efficiency standard or the heating does not work, that is a separate housing issue — Citizens Advice and Shelter can help. A letter about energy is not a reason to leave your home without advice.

Sources: Citizens Advice — Renting a home · Shelter — Repairs in rented housing · Ofgem — Energy advice for households

Spot Fake Laws

Online, you will meet people who claim a phrase, a capitalised spelling, or an old "common law" idea can make a court claim vanish. These are called pseudolegal or "freeman" tactics. They do not work in UK courts, they waste your time, and they can make the outcome worse. Here is how to check anything you are told.

Verify every statute on legislation.gov.uk

If someone quotes an Act, check it exists and is still in force at legislation.gov.uk, the official UK statute database. Energy is governed by real Acts such as the Electricity Act 1989, the Gas Act 1986 and the Utilities Act 2000. Court and enforcement rules include the Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007. Who can act as a lawyer is set by the Legal Services Act 2007. If you cannot find the "Act" someone quotes, it is not law.

Source: legislation.gov.uk — The National Archives · GOV.UK

Statute, guidance, byelaw, terms — they are not equal

A statute (Act of Parliament) is law made by Parliament and can be enforced by courts. Guidance (Ofgem, GOV.UK) explains how rules should be applied but is not itself law. A byelaw is a local rule made by a council or body with Parliament's permission. Terms and conditions are a contract between you and a company — they can be challenged and cannot override statute. If someone mixes these up and calls a contract "the law", check which one it really is.

Source: legislation.gov.uk — Help using legislation

Wartime and temporary Acts: check for sunset clauses

Some old or emergency Acts have expiry dates ("sunset clauses") or have been repealed. Even Ofgem's prepayment warrant protections were originally time-limited and had to be formally extended. Always check the "status" and "more about" notes on legislation.gov.uk to see if an Act is still in force, partially repealed, or expired. A law that ended decades ago cannot help you today.

Source: legislation.gov.uk — How to check if legislation is in force

"Common law" claims and magic words: check, then ignore

Phrases like "I do not consent", "all caps names", or claims that "common law" overrides Parliament are not defences in UK courts. No paperwork removes your duty to respond to a legitimate court claim. The courts have repeatedly rejected these arguments. If a video or post tells you to ignore a court letter or to threaten the other side, that is the moment to walk away and contact Citizens Advice, a solicitor, or LawWorks.

Sources: Judiciary — Guidance and resources · Citizens Advice — Law and courts

Court Prep Checklist

Before you go

  • Bring the letter, your evidence, bills, receipts, any replies you sent, photo ID, and a notebook with dates.
  • Arrive early — aim for 30 minutes before the hearing time. Eat and drink before you go; court days are long.
  • Address the judge as "Sir" or "Madam" (or "Your Honour" in the county court). Speak only when it is your turn. Stay calm and stick to facts.
  • Bring a McKenzie friend if you want one — tell the judge at the start. They can sit with you, take notes, help with papers and quietly advise, but they cannot speak for you, question witnesses, or sign documents. The judge can refuse permission, so be ready to sit alone.
  • A duty solicitor is for criminal cases (police station and magistrates court). For a civil county court energy debt hearing, use Citizens Advice, a LawWorks free clinic, a Law Centre, the Access to Justice Foundation, or Civil Legal Advice (legal aid) on 0345 345 4345.
  • Save these numbers: Citizens Advice consumer 0808 223 1133 · Civil Legal Advice 0345 345 4345 · Energy Ombudsman via energyombudsman.org.
